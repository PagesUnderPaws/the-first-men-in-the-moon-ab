XXV
THE GRAND LUNAR

The penultimate message describes, with occasionally even elaborate detail, the encounter between Cavor and the Grand Lunar, who is the ruler or master of the moon. Cavor seems to have sent most of it without interference, but to have been interrupted in the concluding portion. The second came after an interval of a week.

The first message begins: “At last I am able to resume this—” it then becomes illegible for a space, and after a time resumes in mid-sentence.

The missing words of the following sentence are probably “the crowd.” There follows quite clearly: “grew ever denser as we drew near the palace of the Grand Lunar—if I may call a series of excavations a palace. Everywhere faces stared at me—blank, chitinous gapes and masks, eyes peering over tremendous olfactory developments, eyes beneath monstrous forehead plates; an undergrowth of smaller creatures dodged and yelped, and helmet faces poised on sinuous, long-jointed necks appeared craning over shoulders and beneath armpits. Keeping a welcome space about me marched a cordon of stolid, scuttle-headed guards, who had joined us on our leaving the boat in which we had come along the channels of the Central Sea. The quick-eyed artist with the little brain joined us also, and a thick bunch of lean porter-insects swayed and struggled under the multitude of conveniences that were considered essential to my state. I was carried in a litter during the final stage of our journey. This litter was made of some very ductile metal that looked dark to me, meshed and woven, and with bars of paler metal, and about me as I advanced there grouped itself a long and complicated procession.

“In front, after the manner of heralds, marched four trumpet-faced creatures making a devastating bray; and then came squat, resolute-moving ushers before and behind, and on either hand a galaxy of learned heads, a sort of animated encyclopædia, who were, Phi-oo explained, to stand about the Grand Lunar for purposes of reference. (Not a thing in lunar science, not a point of view or method of thinking, that these wonderful beings did not carry in their heads!) Followed guards and porters, and then Phi-oo’s shivering brain borne also on a litter. Then came Tsi-puff in a slightly less important litter; then myself on a litter of greater elegance than any other, and surrounded by my food and drink attendants. More trumpeters came next, splitting the ear with vehement outcries, and then several big brains, special correspondents one might well call them, or historiographers, charged with the task of observing and remembering every detail of this epoch-making interview. A company of attendants, bearing and dragging banners and masses of scented fungus and curious symbols, vanished in the darkness behind. The way was lined by ushers and officers in caparisons that gleamed like steel, and beyond their line, so far as my eyes could pierce the gloom, the heads of that enormous crowd extended.

“I will own that I am still by no means indurated to the peculiar effect of the Selenite appearance, and to find myself, as it were, adrift on this broad sea of excited entomology was by no means agreeable. Just for a space I had something very like what I should imagine people mean when they speak of the ‘horrors.’ It had come to me before in these lunar caverns, when on occasion I have found myself weaponless and with an undefended back, amidst a crowd of these Selenites, but never quite so vividly. It is, of course, as absolutely irrational a feeling as one could well have, and I hope gradually to subdue it. But just for a moment, as I swept forward into the welter of the vast crowd, it was only by gripping my litter tightly and summoning all my will-power that I succeeded in avoiding an outcry or some such manifestation. It lasted perhaps three minutes; then I had myself in hand again.

“We ascended the spiral of a vertical way for some time and then passed through a series of huge halls, dome-roofed and elaborately decorated. The approach to the Grand Lunar was certainly contrived to give one a vivid impression of his greatness. Each cavern one entered seemed greater and more boldly arched than its predecessor. This effect of progressive size was enhanced by a thin haze of faintly phosphorescent blue incense that thickened as one advanced, and robbed even the nearer figures of clearness. I seemed to advance continually to something larger, dimmer, and less material.

“I must confess that all this multitude made me feel extremely shabby and unworthy. I was unshaven and unkempt; I had brought no razor; I had a coarse beard over my mouth. On earth I have always been inclined to despise any attention to my person beyond a proper care for cleanliness; but under the exceptional circumstances in which I found myself, representing, as I did, my planet and my kind, and depending very largely upon the attractiveness of my appearance for a proper reception, I could have given much for something a little more artistic and dignified than the husks I wore. I had been so serene in the belief that the moon was uninhabited as to overlook such precautions altogether. As it was I was dressed in a flannel jacket, knickerbockers, and golfing stockings, stained with every sort of dirt the moon offered; slippers (of which the left heel was wanting), and a blanket, through a hole in which I thrust my head. (These clothes, indeed, I still wear.) Sharp bristles are anything but an improvement to my cast of features, and there was an unmended tear at the knee of my knickerbockers that showed conspicuously as I squatted in my litter; my right stocking, too, persisted in getting about my ankle. I am fully alive to the injustice my appearance did humanity, and if by any expedient I could have improvised something a little out of the way and imposing I would have done so. But I could hit upon nothing. I did what I could with my blanket—folding it somewhat after the fashion of a toga, and for the rest I sat as upright as the swaying of my litter permitted.

“Imagine the largest hall you have ever been in, imperfectly lit with blue light and obscured by a grey-blue fog, surging with metallic or livid-grey creatures of such a mad diversity as I have hinted. Imagine this hall to end in an open archway beyond which is a still larger hall, and beyond this yet another and still larger one, and so on. At the end of the vista, dimly seen, a flight of steps, like the steps of Ara Cœli at Rome, ascend out of sight. Higher and higher these steps appear to go as one draws nearer their base. But at last I came under a huge archway and beheld the summit of these steps, and upon it the Grand Lunar exalted on his throne.

“He was seated in what was relatively a blaze of incandescent blue. This, and the darkness about him, gave him an effect of floating in a blue-black void. He seemed a small, self-luminous cloud at first, brooding on his sombre throne; his brain case must have measured many yards in diameter. For some reason that I cannot fathom a number of blue search-lights radiated from behind the throne on which he sat, and immediately encircling him was a halo. About him, and little and indistinct in this glow, a number of body-servants sustained and supported him, and overshadowed and standing in a huge semicircle beneath him were his intellectual subordinates, his remembrancers and computators and searchers and servants, and all the distinguished insects of the court of the moon. Still lower stood ushers and messengers, and then all down the countless steps of the throne were guards, and at the base, enormous, various, indistinct, vanishing at last into an absolute black, a vast swaying multitude of the minor dignitaries of the moon. Their feet made a perpetual scraping whisper on the rocky floor, their limbs moved with a rustling murmur.

“As I entered the penultimate hall the music rose and expanded into an imperial magnificence of sound, and the shrieks of the newsbearers died away....

“I entered the last and greatest hall....

“My procession opened out like a fan. My ushers and guards went right and left, and the three litters bearing myself and Phi-oo and Tsi-puff marched across a shiny darkness of floor to the foot of the giant stairs. Then began a vast throbbing hum, that mingled with the music. The two Selenites dismounted, but I was bidden remain seated—I imagine as a special honour. The music ceased, but not that humming, and by a simultaneous movement of ten thousand respectful heads my attention was directed to the enhaloed supreme intelligence that hovered above me.

“At first as I peered into the radiating glow this quintessential brain looked very much like an opaque, featureless bladder with dim, undulating ghosts of convolutions writhing visibly within. Then beneath its enormity and just above the edge of the throne one saw with a start minute elfin eyes peering out of the glow. No face, but eyes, as if they peered through holes. At first I could see no more than these two staring little eyes, and then below I distinguished the little dwarfed body and its insect-jointed limbs shrivelled and white. The eyes stared down at me with a strange intensity, and the lower part of the swollen globe was wrinkled. Ineffectual-looking little hand-tentacles steadied this shape on the throne....

“It was great. It was pitiful. One forgot the hall and the crowd.

“I ascended the staircase by jerks. It seemed to me that this darkly glowing brain case above us spread over me, and took more and more of the whole effect into itself as I drew nearer. The tiers of attendants and helpers grouped about their master seemed to dwindle and fade into the night. I saw that shadowy attendants were busy spraying that great brain with a cooling spray, and patting and sustaining it. For my own part, I sat gripping my swaying litter and staring at the Grand Lunar, unable to turn my gaze aside. And at last, as I reached a little landing that was separated only by ten steps or so from the supreme seat, the woven splendour of the music reached a climax and ceased, and I was left naked, as it were, in that vastness, beneath the still scrutiny of the Grand Lunar’s eyes.

“He was scrutinising the first man he had ever seen....

“My eyes dropped at last from his greatness to the faint figures in the blue mist about him, and then down the steps to the massed Selenites, still and expectant in their thousands, packed on the floor below. Once again an unreasonable horror reached out towards me.... And passed.

“After the pause came the salutation. I was assisted from my litter, and stood awkwardly while a number of curious and no doubt deeply symbolical gestures were vicariously performed for me by two slender officials. The encyclopædic galaxy of the learned that had accompanied me to the entrance of the last hall appeared two steps above me and left and right of me, in readiness for the Grand Lunar’s need, and Phi-oo’s pale brain placed itself about half-way up to the throne in such a position as to communicate easily between us without turning his back on either the Grand Lunar or myself. Tsi-puff took up a position behind him. Dexterous ushers sidled sideways towards me, keeping a full face to the Presence. I seated myself Turkish fashion, and Phi-oo and Tsi-puff also knelt down above me. There came a pause. The eyes of the nearer court went from me to the Grand Lunar and came back to me, and a hissing and piping of expectation passed across the hidden multitudes below and ceased.

“That humming ceased.

“For the first and last time in my experience the moon was silent.

“I became aware of a faint wheezy noise. The Grand Lunar was addressing me. It was like the rubbing of a finger upon a pane of glass.

“I watched him attentively for a time, and then glanced at the alert Phi-oo. I felt amidst these slender beings ridiculously thick and fleshy and solid; my head all jaw and black hair. My eyes went back to the Grand Lunar. He had ceased; his attendants were busy, and his shining superficies was glistening and running with cooling spray.

“Phi-oo meditated through an interval. He consulted Tsi-puff. Then he began piping his recognisable English—at first a little nervously, so that he was not very clear.

“‘M’m—the Grand Lunar—wishes to say—wishes to say—he gathers you are—m’m—men—that you are a man from the planet earth. He wishes to say that he welcomes you—welcomes you—and wishes to learn—learn, if I may use the word—the state of your world, and the reason why you came to this.’

He paused, and this time I answered. I said that I had come chiefly because I wished to know. We had long looked at the moon from the earth without knowing what manner of world it was, and when I had found a means of crossing the space between us it had seemed to me almost impossible not to use it. I was a student of physical science, and discovery itself was sufficient reason for the journey.

The means naturally interested him. On gravitation his conceptions were far more exact than my own; Cavorite was the novelty. I explained that it did not repel a gravitating body but screened bodies upon one side of it from attraction upon the other. I described the sphere and the Cavorite blinds by which we exposed ourselves at will to the attraction of the earth, moon or sun. He wished to know how the effect varied with thickness and distance, what occurred at the edge of a sheet, whether one screen influenced another, and what happened when several attracting bodies acted from different directions. To several of these questions I could give only the answer that I did not know. I had discovered the substance and made use of its principal property; I had by no means exhausted its physics. For a little while our talk became highly technical, but we did not then come to the actual ingredients of the stuff.

He knew, from the reports already before him, that there had been another man—Bedford—and wished to know what function Bedford had performed. I told him Bedford had been my companion in the expedition. He had assisted me in preparing the sphere and had come with me to the moon. This did not altogether satisfy him. What particular office had Bedford performed in the journey?

“None, exactly,” I said. “We came together.”

That answer seemed to present a difficulty of a kind quite different from the difficulty of Cavorite. He allowed it to remain. My own position was easier: I had already told him I was a student of physical science, and I had the impression that for the moment he had found a place in his scheme of things into which I might be fitted.

He understood, he explained, that we lived on the surface of the earth, that our air and sea were outside the globe; the latter part, indeed, he already knew from his astronomical specialists. He was very anxious to have more detailed information of what he called this extraordinary state of affairs. I told him of the changing temperature of our surface, of clouds and rain, of winds and snow, and of the perpetual exposure of all terrestrial life to the heavens. His imagination was assisted by the fact that the lunar atmosphere in the outer galleries of the night side is not infrequently very foggy. He seemed inclined to marvel that we did not find the sunlight too intense for our eyes, and was interested in my attempt to explain that the sky was tempered to a bluish colour through the refraction of the air, though I doubt if he clearly understood that.

I explained how the iris of the human eyes can contract the pupil and save the delicate internal structure from the excess of sunlight, and was allowed to approach within a few feet of the Presence in order that this structure might be seen. This led to a comparison of the lunar and terrestrial eyes. The former is not only excessively sensitive to such light as men can see, but it can also see heat, and every difference in temperature within the moon renders objects visible to it.

The iris was quite a new organ to the Grand Lunar. For a time he amused himself by flashing his rays into my face and watching my pupils contract. As a consequence, I was dazzled and blinded for some little time....

But in spite of that discomfort I found something reassuring by insensible degrees in the rationality of this business of question and answer. I could shut my eyes, think of my answer, and almost forget that the Grand Lunar has no face....

When I had descended again to my proper place he asked how we protected ourselves against heat, cold and storms, and presently why we commonly erected our dwellings upon the surface instead of excavating them. I told him that men had indeed lived in caves and still drove mines, tunnels, railways and other works underground, but that most of our daily business remained above. Light and air could be had there without contrivance; water could be carried away; fields, roads and places of work were upon the surface; and the labour of digging great habitations into rock would often exceed the labour of building them.

Windows detained him for a little while. That we should deliberately pierce the walls which sheltered us seemed at first a contradiction, until I explained glass and our desire for daylight. From windows we came to rooms and separate households, and I had some trouble explaining why men who lived close together should nevertheless divide themselves into innumerable private enclosures. I gave him what reasons I could—warmth, property, family life, privacy, custom—and I fancy at last he accepted the fact rather than the explanation.

The mention of mines led him to ask what we knew of the interior of our globe. Here I was more cautious than I should perhaps have been at the beginning of the interview. Directly, I told him, we knew extraordinarily little. Our deepest workings were trifling scratches upon a body nearly eight thousand miles in diameter. Yet it was not true that we knew nothing. We could reason from effects.

The density of the earth as a whole was greater than that of the rocks accessible at its surface; its figure and rotation, the behaviour of gravitation, the increase of heat with depth, and other observations all imposed conditions upon any account of its interior. I told him that Wiechert, only a few years before, had framed an hypothesis of a dense metallic central region, chiefly iron, surrounded by a lighter stony shell. Earthquakes were beginning to furnish another means of inquiry. Their disturbances could now be recorded at very great distances, and Oldham’s examination of distant records of the great Indian earthquake had distinguished different sorts of motion arriving at different times. These disturbances had travelled through the substance of the earth itself, and their times and courses might therefore tell us something of regions no miner could ever reach.

The Grand Lunar questioned me closely upon the distinction between what had been seen, what had been inferred, and what was still no more than hypothesis. I found the distinction unexpectedly pleasant to make. After some of my earlier explanations of terrestrial custom it was a relief to reach a subject upon which uncertainty could be stated without embarrassment.

He seemed nevertheless amused that men had crossed the space to the moon while four thousand miles of their own planet remained inaccessible to them.

“Empty space,” I said, “is considerably easier to penetrate than solid rock.”

He considered this.

A movement passed through the court which I could only interpret as laughter.

The little disturbance of amusement occasioned by my remark upon empty space had scarcely subsided when one of the learned company was brought forward with a diagram of the earth. He had followed my account of its interior and had contrived, with a rapidity I greatly envied, a sectional drawing of the globe, distinguishing in some fashion what I had represented as known from what was merely inferred or conjectured. I corrected two or three particulars, and from this we wandered into the uses men make of drawing.

The sectional drawing led us from maps and diagrams to pictures made for other purposes. I told him that we drew faces, landscapes, animals and scenes of ordinary life, and that a picture might be made for no more useful purpose than the pleasure of looking at it. I attempted some explanation of painting and sculpture, of likeness and arrangement, and presently found myself involved in distinctions I was quite unable to defend.

“I am not a painter,” I said at last.

That disposed of my difficulties more effectually than anything else I had said.

I had been sitting cross-legged all this while, and at about this point one of my feet became completely numb. In shifting myself I brought upon it that intolerable prickling which follows the return of sensation, and made some movement sufficiently violent to attract attention. I had to explain that nothing serious had happened, and that the human body was merely ill-fitted to remain indefinitely in one posture. The incident led nowhere, which was perhaps fortunate; but in altering my position I exposed more completely the deplorable state of my clothes, and presently we had fallen upon the subject of dress.

From dress we soon passed beyond warmth and protection to decency, rank, custom, and finally fashion. The Grand Lunar pressed me rather closely upon this last matter, wishing to discover what improvement was effected by the continual alteration of garments. I was obliged to admit that a new fashion need be neither warmer, stronger, cheaper nor more convenient than the old, and might indeed be worse in every one of these respects. This produced one of the pauses I had begun to recognise. I explained as well as I could that men desired not merely to be clothed but to appear properly clothed in the eyes of other men; that the standard altered; that novelty itself counted for something; and that a shape which at one time seemed ridiculous might presently become admirable, and in due course ridiculous again.

I do not think he was greatly impressed by fashion.

Ornaments were easier. Whatever else might be said of lunar society, I had only to look about me to see that ceremony, rank and display were no mysteries there. We compared, in a loose way, their insignia and processional splendours with our jewels, medals and decorations, and I remember thinking that vanity, at least, seemed unlikely to prove an exclusively terrestrial possession. The cooling spray descended twice upon the Grand Lunar during this part of our talk, and for a little while his enormous superficies glistened through a drifting bluish mist. It recalled me with some force to my surroundings. I had become so accustomed to answering questions that at moments I forgot altogether the monstrous intelligence above me, the ranks of specialised heads, and the unseen multitude fading away into darkness below.

The music that had accompanied my approach to the Presence supplied our next comparison. The Grand Lunar wished to know whether men also arranged sounds for ceremony and pleasure. I told him of singing and instruments, of orchestras and solitary performers, and for a time we compared, so far as Phi-oo could manage it, some of our different means of producing ordered sound. Presently, however, he turned from the means to the effects, and questioned me upon the curious power music has over men—why one arrangement of sounds should delight and another distress us, and why a succession of notes might induce melancholy, excitement or tranquillity when nothing corresponding had happened. Of this I could give him very little account. I knew that habit and association entered into the matter, and that tastes differed widely; but beyond such generalities I was soon at the end of my resources. I confessed that although I enjoyed music I had never made a study of it.

This, I noticed, interested him.

An attendant brought me food and something to drink shortly afterwards. My throat was already dry from so much speaking, and I was glad enough of the interruption. The sight of me eating turned the conversation upon the preparation of food. I told the Grand Lunar how extensively men altered what they ate—by boiling, baking, roasting, brewing and seasoning—and of the extraordinary differences of taste between one country and another. Something relished by one people might disgust another, and tastes acquired almost unconsciously in childhood might remain throughout life. He presently asked whether I myself understood these operations. I admitted that although I knew something of them in principle, I could cook only very badly.

I said there was nothing remarkable in that.

From food and custom our talk ranged very widely. I cannot now recover half the turns it took. We touched upon games, ceremonies, the differing habits of men in different countries, and at some point upon dancing. I explained that men and women moved rhythmically to music, sometimes according to fixed figures and sometimes more freely, for amusement, ceremony, courtship, display and a number of other purposes. I myself, I admitted, was no dancer; nevertheless I could recognise dancing and form some judgment of whether it was well or badly done.

It was after this, I think, that the Grand Lunar returned suddenly to something I had said at the beginning. Was physical science not my particular study?

“Chiefly,” I said.

He recalled, in effect, that I had since spoken upon pictures, dress, ornament, music, cookery, games, dancing and a number of other matters which plainly had nothing to do with my science. Were these also studies of mine?

“Good heavens, no!”

For once the exclamation survived Phi-oo almost intact. I explained that I knew scarcely anything of some of these subjects when compared with the men who gave their lives to them. I could not paint, play an instrument, make clothes, cook an elaborate dinner or dance tolerably; but there was nothing unusual in possessing some knowledge of all these things. Men acquired continually a quantity of knowledge beyond the needs of their particular work—from parents and teachers, from companions and strangers, by observation and conversation, and merely through living among other men occupied with different affairs. This seemed to suggest to the Grand Lunar an enormous labour of instruction. He supposed that specialists must spend a great part of their lives teaching fragments of their knowledge to persons who had no particular use for it.

“Oh no!” I said. “A great deal of it comes from books.”

I saw at once that this required explanation. I pointed to the sectional drawing of the earth which still lay before the learned Selenite. They already understood how a thing might be represented by marks instead of being present itself; but if every occasion upon which one wished to signify the earth demanded another elaborate drawing of the whole globe, the labour of repetition would soon become intolerable. With us the marks had been carried much farther towards abbreviation. A small number of conventional signs stood for the sounds of speech, and by their combinations we could set down almost anything that might be said. A man might thus put an observation or an idea into marks which another man, seeing them afterwards, could turn again into words. Printing made the repetition easier still, so that the same words might be multiplied hundreds or thousands of times.

“And these marks remain?”

“Certainly.”

“For how long?”

“For centuries, sometimes.”

He asked about the trustworthiness of such records, and I explained that permanence gave no guarantee of truth. An error might be printed as readily as a fact; books contradicted one another; conclusions were disputed and corrected; and much survived in print which nobody any longer believed.

This seemed curious enough to him, but a greater difficulty arose when I happened to mention that some books were not intended to be true at all. At first he understood me to mean false histories or deliberate deceptions, and I had considerable trouble in making clear what I meant by fiction. A writer, I explained, invented persons and events, and his readers followed their adventures while perfectly aware that none of them had occurred. Phi-oo became entangled here and had to consult Tsi-puff more than once. When at last the distinction between a lie and an acknowledged invention had been established, the Grand Lunar seized upon the matter with admirable directness.

“Yet wishes to know what happened next?”

“Very much.”

I confess that put so baldly the reading of fiction appeared less reasonable than I had ever supposed it. I attempted some defence involving character, probability, expectation and the pleasure of imagined events; but I doubt whether I made much of it. Poetry, which I introduced afterwards, only increased the confusion.

Then Bedford came unexpectedly into the matter. I mentioned that before our departure he had been occupied with the idea of writing a play, and this obliged me to explain the theatre. I told them that actors represented other persons before an audience, speaking words prepared beforehand and behaving for a time as though imagined events were actually occurring. The Grand Lunar wished to know whether the spectators were deceived, and when I told him they were not, whether deception was at least intended by the actors. My denial of that left him with the still stranger spectacle of a whole assembly knowingly assisting at a pretence for the pleasure of doing so.

This amused him exceedingly.

I retorted that the Selenites themselves had ceremony, music and symbolical performances which could scarcely be reduced altogether to utility; and upon that we became involved in a comparison whose course I can no longer recover.

By now my legs were stiff, my mouth dry again, and I had begun to feel the peculiar exhaustion that comes from prolonged attention. The blue light, which at first had seemed only strange, tired my eyes; my back ached from sitting; and more than once I caught myself shifting my weight while Phi-oo was still disentangling some point I had made several sentences before. It occurred to me that we must have been talking for hours. Yet the interview had lost none of its fascination for me. The questions wandered, doubled back, struck suddenly into matters I had never thought difficult, and continually exposed assumptions I had carried all my life without knowing they were assumptions.

When the Grand Lunar presently began another inquiry I found myself, in spite of my stiffness and fatigue, leaning forward once more to hear him.

It was perhaps my fatigue rather than anything in the argument that determined the next turn of our conversation. I had shifted myself repeatedly by this time, and during one of Phi-oo’s more laborious renderings I gave an enormous yawn. The Grand Lunar noticed it immediately and wished to know what the movement signified. I told him that I was becoming tired, and that before very long I should have to sleep. This interested him when he understood that sleep was not with us merely an occasional consequence of exceptional exertion, but a necessity returning with great regularity. He wished to know whether I must sleep then, and I told him not immediately; an answer which, I remember, seemed considerably less satisfactory to my body than to the Grand Lunar.

From sleep we came again to the alternation of day and night, and thence to those creatures which are chiefly abroad after sunset. I told him of owls and bats, of wolves, lions and tigers, and of animals which hunted other animals for food. He understood readily enough the danger of such creatures; the moon had formidable life in its waters, and the Selenites knew force and restraint. But he returned more than once to the extraordinary fact, as it seemed to him, that great numbers of terrestrial creatures lived wholly outside human ordering and pursued their own affairs without reference to us. From these wild creatures we came naturally to those men had brought under control, and thence to the fields and pastures spread over the surface of the earth. I spoke of harvests, herds, and the immense tracts from which great cities drew their food. Their exposure to weather, and the dependence of millions of men upon distant fields and pastures, kept his questions moving for some time. I found myself speaking of these matters with considerably more assurance than my knowledge justified, and escaped at last by reminding him that they were not my profession.

Then, quite suddenly, he returned to Bedford. He had not forgotten the difficulty he had left unsettled earlier, and now wished to know what Bedford ordinarily did upon the earth. This was less easy to answer briefly than I had expected. I said first that Bedford was a man of business.

That conveyed very little.

I had then to explain that he concerned himself with buying and selling, with undertakings entered upon for profit, and with discovering occasions by which money might be made into more money. It was in this connexion, I told him, that Bedford had regarded Cavorite very differently from myself. To me it had been principally a physical discovery; to him it suggested patents, factories, transport, companies, monopolies and wealth. The Grand Lunar considered all this for some time, and I was obliged to explain money.

I described money as something generally accepted in exchange, and from this we came to prices, property, wages, trade and companies. A man might possess a share in an undertaking he had never seen, receive money because it prospered, lose money because it failed, and sell his interest in it without ever laying a hand upon the actual work. Bedford’s particular gift, I explained, lay in perceiving such opportunities and acting upon them promptly. I called it, for want of a better expression, a practical imagination.

This brought the Grand Lunar no nearer to understanding why Bedford had accompanied me to the moon. Had his presence been necessary to my work? I said it had not. Had he been ordered to come by some authority?

“No,” I said. “He chose to come.”

There was some difficulty over this. The Grand Lunar appeared at first to suppose that Bedford had merely been allowed to choose between several duties. I explained that no duty had been laid upon him at all. He had wished to make the journey and had made it.

Presently he asked who determined what work men were to do upon the earth.

I told him that, for the most part, no one did. Men entered occupations, sought employment, established businesses, inherited positions, abandoned one pursuit for another, and in innumerable ways chose or stumbled into the work by which they lived.

This answer plainly disturbed his conception of terrestrial affairs. He reminded me of the fields and herds I had described, of the multitudes living in cities, and of all the labour by which food and other necessities passed from one to the other. If no directing intelligence assigned these functions, how was so vast a business ordered?

I tried to explain that much of it was not ordered in that sense at all. A farmer might grow wheat because he expected someone would buy it; another man bought it because he expected to sell it again; others carried it, ground it, baked it and sold the bread, each concerned chiefly with his own part and often knowing almost nothing of the rest. Money, custom, law, necessity and private advantage held these separate activities together well enough for the whole to continue.

The Grand Lunar considered this for some time.

“Then who directs the whole?”

“No one.”

That answer required repetition.

There were authorities, I explained, but none corresponding to the Grand Lunar himself. Towns and districts had their officers; nations had governments; laws were made and enforced; but no single intelligence directed mankind or even understood the whole movement of its affairs.

What, then, stood above the government of Britain?

I told him nothing did. Britain was only one power among many.

“But these governments are all human?”

I said they were.

He wished to know whether one of them governed the rest. I said no. There were many independent States and empires, differing enormously in extent and power, making treaties, exchanging ambassadors and managing their dealings with one another as best they could. There were rules and customs between them, but no authority above them in the same sense that a government stood above its own people. The Grand Lunar considered this for a while and then asked what happened when two such powers desired things that could not both be had. I told him they might argue, threaten, yield, compromise—or fight. He did not dwell upon weapons or upon force. Instead he returned again and again to the fact that one body of men, acting under one government, might be deliberately set against another body of men acting under another, with no common authority above them. I told him of armies and navies, frontiers, disputed lands, commerce, dynastic claims, fear, rivalry, alliances, and the innumerable causes by which governments come to persuade themselves that fighting has become necessary. I tried also, and I think rather badly, to explain patriotism—the attachment of men to their own country, its history and symbols, and the readiness of many to suffer or die in its service.

He asked whether armies existed only while fighting was actually in progress. I told him that most great States kept them in peace as well. Why? Because other States did the same. And why did the others? Because they too feared their neighbours, or wished to be prepared against them.

This occupied him for a little while, during which the attendants sprayed his brow. I asked, partly to escape the direction in which the questions were tending, whether disputes never arose among the Selenites. Of course they did. Individuals might disobey; creatures became dangerous; mistakes were made; restraint and force were sometimes necessary. But all these things happened within one order. There was no lunar equivalent, so far as I could understand him, of several supreme authorities confronting one another, each with nowhere higher to which either could appeal. The difference, then, was not that force was unknown to them, but that upon the earth authority came to an end in several places. There were many governments, and beyond them no government at all.

The Grand Lunar wished to know whether these separate States were expected always to remain separate. I said there were men who desired larger unions, others who believed nations would endure indefinitely, and still others who scarcely troubled themselves with the matter until some immediate quarrel forced it upon them.

“And which do you expect?”

“I do not know,” I said.

The cooling spray hissed again above me. I shifted my aching knee and stretched one foot as far as dignity permitted. After a little pause the Grand Lunar asked how deep the terrestrial oceans were.

I confess the change of subject relieved me.

After Bedford, companies, governments, armies and all the accumulated complication of mankind, it was agreeable to find myself once more among matters that could be measured.

The question of the oceans detained us for some time. I tried to give him some notion of their extent—vast tracts of water reaching beyond the horizon in every direction, spread upon the outside of the globe beneath the open sky. I spoke of coasts thousands of miles in length, of winds raising waves against which even our largest ships must contend, and of depths known to us chiefly by soundings and by what could be dredged from below. The deeper waters passed beyond the reach of sunlight and descended into perpetual darkness; and from these regions men had brought up living things of forms sufficiently strange even to persons accustomed to the extravagances of terrestrial life.

At some mention of their own water creatures I told him of whales—air-breathing animals which nevertheless spend nearly all their lives in the sea—of fishes ranging from minute things to sharks of formidable size, and of creatures that drifted, crawled, burrowed, swam or remained fixed upon rocks. The Central Sea supplied comparisons enough for Phi-oo to keep pace with me. I touched also upon animals passing between water and land, and upon the immense antiquity and change through which terrestrial life had assumed so many shapes; but upon this last matter I was cautious, for I knew enough of biology to know how little authority I had in it.

Harbours brought in the tides. I described the gradual uncovering of rocks and mud-flats, the filling and emptying of estuaries, and boats floating at one hour and resting upon mud at another. The Grand Lunar’s questions soon warned me against venturing too freely into the theory. It struck me, while I spoke, as an exceedingly odd thing that I should be sitting inside the moon describing to its ruler the daily consequences of lunar attraction upon an earthly shore; and something in my manner must have conveyed the absurdity, for the Grand Lunar appeared to share my amusement.

Ships led naturally to the difficulty of finding one’s course across great stretches with no cavern wall, roof, fixed channel or landmark in sight. I described the compass, charts, observations of the sun and stars, and the determination of latitude and longitude. Longitude brought in chronometers, and thence the peculiar exactness with which civilised men divided their ordinary days. I had already discovered that the Selenites counted by years, though their numerals still defeated me, so I confined myself chiefly to our uses of hours and minutes. A man in London might regulate his rising, his breakfast, his departure, his railway journey, his work and his meeting with another man almost to the minute.

From travellers we somehow came to languages. I told him that mankind possessed not one language but a great number, some closely akin and others so different that the speakers of one could understand nothing of another. With Phi-oo between us there was little point in discussing the expedients by which men crossed such divisions; the Grand Lunar returned instead several times to the fact that these mutually unintelligible tongues all belonged to mankind. I told him that languages changed as peoples separated, mingled and altered; that the same tongue varied from place to place and from century to century; and that old speech might become strange even to descendants of those who had spoken it.

He wished to know whether men had attempted to establish a common language. I said there had been schemes enough, but that speech was not a contrivance which could simply be thrown aside when a more convenient one was devised. A language was learnt before a man was old enough to reason about it; it became entangled with his earliest memories, his habits, his literature and the people among whom he lived. By this time the Grand Lunar had become somewhat accustomed to finding that many earthly things had come about without anybody intending them, and he accepted this more readily than I think he would have done at the beginning of our conversation.

I cannot now recover the order of all that followed. The interview had by then lost anything resembling a formal sequence. One answer suggested another question; some phrase recalled a matter abandoned half an hour before; and the Grand Lunar would pursue an incidental point until I had nearly forgotten the subject from which it had arisen. We touched upon calendars and the division of the year, upon names, upon customs surviving after the reasons for them had been forgotten, and upon differences between one part of mankind and another which had nothing whatever to do with government. More than once I found myself beginning an explanation only to discover that I had given some portion of it much earlier.

Navigation had brought the heavens repeatedly into our talk, and presently the Grand Lunar returned to terrestrial astronomy. He wished to know rather more exactly what men had seen of the moon before our coming. I described our telescopes and the maps constructed from them, the mountains, plains and great circular formations whose changing shadows had occupied generations of observers. He recalled, with evident amusement, my statement at the beginning of the audience that we had commonly supposed the moon to be dead.

I defended our astronomers.

Nothing visible from the earth had betrayed the world beneath the surface. We saw no clouds, seas, forests or obvious atmosphere; no moving multitude or artificial light. The great works of the Selenites, their cities, galleries and the whole prodigious life through which I had travelled were hidden beneath the crust of the moon. Men had reasoned from what they could see, and if their conclusion had been wrong it had at least been a reasonable error.

This appeared to satisfy him, though not sufficiently to prevent his finding our mistake entertaining.

I tried then to describe the moon as it appeared to terrestrial eyes—not merely as an object of astronomy, but as something continually present in ordinary life; rising over roofs and hills, shining upon fields and water, changing its apparent form from night to night, and entering into calendars, stories, superstitions and poetry. I ventured upon one or two of the figurative expressions men habitually apply to moonlight, but Phi-oo began almost immediately to demand an exact correspondence between the words and the physical thing, and I abandoned the attempt before we found ourselves once more involved in fiction.

For a time after this our positions were pleasantly reversed, and the Grand Lunar caused Phi-oo to tell me something of the earth as it appeared to lunar observers. Their astronomers had watched the movements of cloud upon its surface, distinguished the great divisions of land and water, and followed changes no terrestrial observer could ever see from quite the same point of view. I was astonished by the accuracy of some conclusions they had drawn at so great a distance and no less delighted by others which were entirely mistaken.

There is, I confess, a peculiar satisfaction in correcting the astronomers of another world.

This part of the audience refreshed me considerably. I had spent hours reducing the familiar earth to explanations and definitions, and now for the first time I heard it spoken of as a remote body in the heavens. It was extraordinarily dislocating to think that England and all the countries beyond it, the seas, the little houses and fields, all mankind with its loves and quarrels, its businesses, governments and ambitions, were gathered there upon one distant globe. Everything that to us seemed separate and enormous—nations, cities, journeys, wars, the whole clamour of human affairs—was from the moon compressed into that small bright world, half hidden beneath its wandering clouds. I remember for a little while feeling not so much far from the earth as outside it.

After that my recollection becomes increasingly fragmentary. We spoke of the other planets, of what terrestrial astronomers had observed or inferred concerning them, of eclipses and comets, and of a number of lesser matters which have left no clear order in my memory. Sometimes the Grand Lunar questioned me closely; sometimes he spoke for a considerable interval and Phi-oo gave me only the substance of his remarks.

I had ceased by then to feel that I was being examined.

It had become, in spite of every grotesque circumstance of our surroundings, very nearly a conversation.

My body was less accommodating than my mind. My back had ached for a long time, my knees were stiff, and the repeated shifting of my position gave only temporary relief. My mouth remained dry in spite of the drink they had supplied me, and the bluish illumination, after so many hours, had begun to weary my eyes. Once, during a particularly elaborate passage from Phi-oo, I realised that I had been listening to the sounds of his English without attaching any meaning to them whatever, and had to ask him to begin again. The Grand Lunar himself had been sprayed and tended continually; attendants adjusted the supports about him, and even that vast intelligence seemed subject to bodily limits.

At length there was a movement among the officials nearest the Presence, and I understood that the audience was to end. The Grand Lunar conveyed that there remained much he wished to learn concerning the earth. I replied that there remained incomparably more I wished to learn of the moon, and gathered that our conversation was to be resumed.

There followed another ceremonial performance whose meaning escaped me almost completely. The court stirred, the officials moved into their appointed places, and I attempted to rise. After sitting so long my legs proved incapable at first of anything resembling dignified locomotion, and for a few moments I stood in a most ridiculous attitude while sensation returned to them. Two attendants assisted me to my litter, and presently I was being carried away from the Presence through the blue-lit succession of galleries.

I went from the Grand Lunar in a condition of extraordinary mental exhilaration. For all the grotesqueness of his form and all the profound strangeness of the society over which he ruled, I had found in him something I had scarcely dared hope to find when I entered that enormous hall—an intelligence with which one could reason. We had misunderstood one another continually, but the misunderstandings themselves could be examined, corrected and made the starting-points of fresh questions. I had gone before him as a captured curiosity from another world and came away, or so at least it seemed to me then, almost as a visiting philosopher.

I remember thinking, as they carried me back through the blue obscurity, that if Bedford had only remained with me long enough to see this, even he might have revised his opinion of the Selenites.

For my own part, I looked forward eagerly to speaking with the Grand Lunar again.

XXVI
THE LAST MESSAGE CAVOR SENT TO THE EARTH

For several days after the message I have just given we received nothing from Cavor.

There is no reason in that circumstance alone to suppose that anything had happened to him. His opportunities of reaching the apparatus had always been irregular, and more than once an interval of considerable length had occurred between his communications. Nevertheless, after that account of the Grand Lunar, the silence impressed me disagreeably. He had ended in so confident a spirit, so plainly anticipating further intercourse, that I found myself waiting for the continuation with an impatience I had not felt before.

When at last the signals returned they were exceedingly difficult to read.

I have already spoken of the disturbances which from time to time complicated Cavor’s transmissions. They now became much more considerable. Broad undulations crossed and confused the finer movements from which his message had to be recovered, sometimes leaving a few words clear, sometimes obliterating whole passages. Whether these disturbances proceeded accidentally from some lunar apparatus, whether they were connected in any way with Cavor’s own operations, or whether they were deliberately directed against his signals, I cannot say. There was evidence enough to suggest any of these explanations and not enough, so far as I can judge, to establish one.

For a time Mr. Wendigee and I continued our efforts to disentangle the record. Here and there a word or part of a sentence emerged, but nothing from which I could recover with certainty what had happened since Cavor left the presence of the Grand Lunar. Then the disturbances became predominant altogether, and the message was lost.

After that came silence again.

It lasted several days.

I confess that by this time I had begun to read Cavor’s former messages with a new attention. I went over his account of the audience again and again, trying to imagine what might have followed it, and each time found myself less able to decide. There were so many things in that extraordinary conversation which, carried a little farther, might have assumed another aspect than they had at first. Yet I could discover nothing in the record upon which I was prepared to fix the meaning of what came afterwards.

And then suddenly, like a cry in the night, came the last message.

It is the briefest fragment, the broken beginnings of two sentences.

The first was:

“I was mad to let the Grand Lunar know—”

There was an interval of perhaps a minute.

What passed during that minute no man can tell. Whether Cavor had left the instrument, whether he was interrupted, whether he hesitated over what he had begun to say, or whether the interruption lay merely in the transmission, are things upon which conjecture is useless.

Then, very rapidly, came:

“Cavorite made as follows: take—”

There followed one word, quite meaningless as it stands:

“uless.”

And that is all.

What it was that Cavor had told the Grand Lunar and now repented telling him, the broken message does not say. I have sometimes fancied I understood him, and at other times the same explanation has seemed to me altogether insufficient. Nor can I tell why, after that first confession, his thoughts turned so urgently to the making of Cavorite. The two fragments stand together, and beyond that I know nothing.

Whatever was happening about that apparatus, no intelligible signal ever reached us again.

For my own part I cannot rid myself of a vision that has grown out of these last broken words: Cavor somewhere in the blue obscurity of the moon, suddenly possessed by the knowledge of some consequence he had never foreseen, hurrying back to his instrument while there was yet time, striving to send across the void one last thing that seemed to him essential. I see him bending over the apparatus, intent upon the wavering signals, perhaps hearing already some movement in the galleries beyond him, perhaps hearing nothing at all.

Then the message breaks.

What followed I cannot know. I see at times the little figure of him turning from the useless apparatus, looking back once towards it, and disappearing among those vast blue-lit passages and the innumerable shapes of that strange world—passing beyond every question we can ask of him, beyond every possibility of answer, into the Unknown—into the dark, into that silence that has no end....
