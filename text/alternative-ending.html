XXV
THE GRAND LUNAR

The penultimate message describes, with occasionally even elaborate detail, the encounter between Cavor and the Grand Lunar, who is the ruler or master of the moon. Cavor seems to have sent most of it without interference, but to have been interrupted in the concluding portion. The second came after an interval of a week.

The first message begins: “At last I am able to resume this—” it then becomes illegible for a space, and after a time resumes in mid-sentence.

The missing words of the following sentence are probably “the crowd.” There follows quite clearly: “grew ever denser as we drew near the palace of the Grand Lunar—if I may call a series of excavations a palace. Everywhere faces stared at me—blank, chitinous gapes and masks, eyes peering over tremendous olfactory developments, eyes beneath monstrous forehead plates; an undergrowth of smaller creatures dodged and yelped, and helmet faces poised on sinuous, long-jointed necks appeared craning over shoulders and beneath armpits. Keeping a welcome space about me marched a cordon of stolid, scuttle-headed guards, who had joined us on our leaving the boat in which we had come along the channels of the Central Sea. The quick-eyed artist with the little brain joined us also, and a thick bunch of lean porter-insects swayed and struggled under the multitude of conveniences that were considered essential to my state. I was carried in a litter during the final stage of our journey. This litter was made of some very ductile metal that looked dark to me, meshed and woven, and with bars of paler metal, and about me as I advanced there grouped itself a long and complicated procession.

“In front, after the manner of heralds, marched four trumpet-faced creatures making a devastating bray; and then came squat, resolute-moving ushers before and behind, and on either hand a galaxy of learned heads, a sort of animated encyclopædia, who were, Phi-oo explained, to stand about the Grand Lunar for purposes of reference. (Not a thing in lunar science, not a point of view or method of thinking, that these wonderful beings did not carry in their heads!) Followed guards and porters, and then Phi-oo’s shivering brain borne also on a litter. Then came Tsi-puff in a slightly less important litter; then myself on a litter of greater elegance than any other, and surrounded by my food and drink attendants. More trumpeters came next, splitting the ear with vehement outcries, and then several big brains, special correspondents one might well call them, or historiographers, charged with the task of observing and remembering every detail of this epoch-making interview. A company of attendants, bearing and dragging banners and masses of scented fungus and curious symbols, vanished in the darkness behind. The way was lined by ushers and officers in caparisons that gleamed like steel, and beyond their line, so far as my eyes could pierce the gloom, the heads of that enormous crowd extended.

“I will own that I am still by no means indurated to the peculiar effect of the Selenite appearance, and to find myself, as it were, adrift on this broad sea of excited entomology was by no means agreeable. Just for a space I had something very like what I should imagine people mean when they speak of the ‘horrors.’ It had come to me before in these lunar caverns, when on occasion I have found myself weaponless and with an undefended back, amidst a crowd of these Selenites, but never quite so vividly. It is, of course, as absolutely irrational a feeling as one could well have, and I hope gradually to subdue it. But just for a moment, as I swept forward into the welter of the vast crowd, it was only by gripping my litter tightly and summoning all my will-power that I succeeded in avoiding an outcry or some such manifestation. It lasted perhaps three minutes; then I had myself in hand again.

“We ascended the spiral of a vertical way for some time and then passed through a series of huge halls, dome-roofed and elaborately decorated. The approach to the Grand Lunar was certainly contrived to give one a vivid impression of his greatness. Each cavern one entered seemed greater and more boldly arched than its predecessor. This effect of progressive size was enhanced by a thin haze of faintly phosphorescent blue incense that thickened as one advanced, and robbed even the nearer figures of clearness. I seemed to advance continually to something larger, dimmer, and less material.

“I must confess that all this multitude made me feel extremely shabby and unworthy. I was unshaven and unkempt; I had brought no razor; I had a coarse beard over my mouth. On earth I have always been inclined to despise any attention to my person beyond a proper care for cleanliness; but under the exceptional circumstances in which I found myself, representing, as I did, my planet and my kind, and depending very largely upon the attractiveness of my appearance for a proper reception, I could have given much for something a little more artistic and dignified than the husks I wore. I had been so serene in the belief that the moon was uninhabited as to overlook such precautions altogether. As it was I was dressed in a flannel jacket, knickerbockers, and golfing stockings, stained with every sort of dirt the moon offered; slippers (of which the left heel was wanting), and a blanket, through a hole in which I thrust my head. (These clothes, indeed, I still wear.) Sharp bristles are anything but an improvement to my cast of features, and there was an unmended tear at the knee of my knickerbockers that showed conspicuously as I squatted in my litter; my right stocking, too, persisted in getting about my ankle. I am fully alive to the injustice my appearance did humanity, and if by any expedient I could have improvised something a little out of the way and imposing I would have done so. But I could hit upon nothing. I did what I could with my blanket—folding it somewhat after the fashion of a toga, and for the rest I sat as upright as the swaying of my litter permitted.

“Imagine the largest hall you have ever been in, imperfectly lit with blue light and obscured by a grey-blue fog, surging with metallic or livid-grey creatures of such a mad diversity as I have hinted. Imagine this hall to end in an open archway beyond which is a still larger hall, and beyond this yet another and still larger one, and so on. At the end of the vista, dimly seen, a flight of steps, like the steps of Ara Cœli at Rome, ascend out of sight. Higher and higher these steps appear to go as one draws nearer their base. But at last I came under a huge archway and beheld the summit of these steps, and upon it the Grand Lunar exalted on his throne.

“He was seated in what was relatively a blaze of incandescent blue. This, and the darkness about him, gave him an effect of floating in a blue-black void. He seemed a small, self-luminous cloud at first, brooding on his sombre throne; his brain case must have measured many yards in diameter. For some reason that I cannot fathom a number of blue search-lights radiated from behind the throne on which he sat, and immediately encircling him was a halo. About him, and little and indistinct in this glow, a number of body-servants sustained and supported him, and overshadowed and standing in a huge semicircle beneath him were his intellectual subordinates, his remembrancers and computators and searchers and servants, and all the distinguished insects of the court of the moon. Still lower stood ushers and messengers, and then all down the countless steps of the throne were guards, and at the base, enormous, various, indistinct, vanishing at last into an absolute black, a vast swaying multitude of the minor dignitaries of the moon. Their feet made a perpetual scraping whisper on the rocky floor, their limbs moved with a rustling murmur.

“As I entered the penultimate hall the music rose and expanded into an imperial magnificence of sound, and the shrieks of the newsbearers died away....

“I entered the last and greatest hall....

“My procession opened out like a fan. My ushers and guards went right and left, and the three litters bearing myself and Phi-oo and Tsi-puff marched across a shiny darkness of floor to the foot of the giant stairs. Then began a vast throbbing hum, that mingled with the music. The two Selenites dismounted, but I was bidden remain seated—I imagine as a special honour. The music ceased, but not that humming, and by a simultaneous movement of ten thousand respectful heads my attention was directed to the enhaloed supreme intelligence that hovered above me.

“At first as I peered into the radiating glow this quintessential brain looked very much like an opaque, featureless bladder with dim, undulating ghosts of convolutions writhing visibly within. Then beneath its enormity and just above the edge of the throne one saw with a start minute elfin eyes peering out of the glow. No face, but eyes, as if they peered through holes. At first I could see no more than these two staring little eyes, and then below I distinguished the little dwarfed body and its insect-jointed limbs shrivelled and white. The eyes stared down at me with a strange intensity, and the lower part of the swollen globe was wrinkled. Ineffectual-looking little hand-tentacles steadied this shape on the throne....

“It was great. It was pitiful. One forgot the hall and the crowd.

“I ascended the staircase by jerks. It seemed to me that this darkly glowing brain case above us spread over me, and took more and more of the whole effect into itself as I drew nearer. The tiers of attendants and helpers grouped about their master seemed to dwindle and fade into the night. I saw that shadowy attendants were busy spraying that great brain with a cooling spray, and patting and sustaining it. For my own part, I sat gripping my swaying litter and staring at the Grand Lunar, unable to turn my gaze aside. And at last, as I reached a little landing that was separated only by ten steps or so from the supreme seat, the woven splendour of the music reached a climax and ceased, and I was left naked, as it were, in that vastness, beneath the still scrutiny of the Grand Lunar’s eyes.

“He was scrutinising the first man he had ever seen....

“My eyes dropped at last from his greatness to the faint figures in the blue mist about him, and then down the steps to the massed Selenites, still and expectant in their thousands, packed on the floor below. Once again an unreasonable horror reached out towards me.... And passed.

“After the pause came the salutation. I was assisted from my litter, and stood awkwardly while a number of curious and no doubt deeply symbolical gestures were vicariously performed for me by two slender officials. The encyclopædic galaxy of the learned that had accompanied me to the entrance of the last hall appeared two steps above me and left and right of me, in readiness for the Grand Lunar’s need, and Phi-oo’s pale brain placed itself about half-way up to the throne in such a position as to communicate easily between us without turning his back on either the Grand Lunar or myself. Tsi-puff took up a position behind him. Dexterous ushers sidled sideways towards me, keeping a full face to the Presence. I seated myself Turkish fashion, and Phi-oo and Tsi-puff also knelt down above me. There came a pause. The eyes of the nearer court went from me to the Grand Lunar and came back to me, and a hissing and piping of expectation passed across the hidden multitudes below and ceased.

“That humming ceased.

“For the first and last time in my experience the moon was silent.

“I became aware of a faint wheezy noise. The Grand Lunar was addressing me. It was like the rubbing of a finger upon a pane of glass.

“I watched him attentively for a time, and then glanced at the alert Phi-oo. I felt amidst these slender beings ridiculously thick and fleshy and solid; my head all jaw and black hair. My eyes went back to the Grand Lunar. He had ceased; his attendants were busy, and his shining superficies was glistening and running with cooling spray.

“Phi-oo meditated through an interval. He consulted Tsi-puff. Then he began piping his recognisable English—at first a little nervously, so that he was not very clear.

“‘M’m—the Grand Lunar—wishes to say—wishes to say—he gathers you are—m’m—men—that you are a man from the planet earth. He wishes to say that he welcomes you—welcomes you—and wishes to learn—learn, if I may use the word—the state of your world, and the reason why you came to this.’

“He paused, and this time I answered. I said that I had come chiefly because I wished to know. We had looked at the moon for ages without knowing what manner of world it was, and when I found myself possessed of a means of crossing the intervening space it had seemed to me almost impossible not to use it. I was, I explained, a student of physical science, and the mere possibility of discovery had been reason enough for me.

“At the mention of the means by which we had come, the Grand Lunar interrupted Phi-oo, and I confess I warmed to the subject. I explained the screening action of Cavorite, the sphere and its blinds, and how, by exposing ourselves now to one gravitating body and now to another, we had steered our way through space. Once fairly started I went on, perhaps rather too eagerly, to indicate what such a substance might do in the moon—the raising of burdens in those prodigious shafts, the easing of passage from level to level, and, beyond these nearer uses, the possibility of voyages from the moon to other worlds. When Phi-oo had translated this the Grand Lunar made him go back over part of it, and for a while the questions came so closely that I had scarcely finished one answer before another was put to me. We talked of gravitation and movement, though we did not then approach the actual composition of the stuff.

“From the sphere he passed naturally to its other occupant. He had learnt already that another man had come with me, and wished to know what part Bedford had played in the enterprise. I said he had been my companion, that he had assisted me in preparing the sphere, and that we had made the journey together. This did not satisfy him. What particular function had Bedford discharged?

“‘None, exactly,’ I said. ‘We came together.’

“That answer presented a difficulty of quite another order from the difficulty of Cavorite. He allowed it to remain. My own position caused no such delay, and presently he passed on to the earth itself.

“I cannot now pretend to give in their proper order all the twistings and turnings of that long interrogation. It lasted, I think, for several hours, and ranged at last over an extraordinary multitude of matters. I can set down only such portions as remain most distinctly in my memory.

“He understood that we lived upon the surface of the earth, that our air and sea were outside the globe, and he was very anxious for particulars of what he called this extraordinary state of affairs. I told him of changing temperature, clouds and rain, winds and snow, and of the perpetual exposure of terrestrial life to the heavens. He marvelled that sunlight did not overpower our eyes. I explained as well as I could the tempering of the light by the atmosphere and the contraction of the iris, and I was permitted to approach the Presence in order that this structure might be inspected. The lunar eye, I learnt in return, is sensitive not only to such light as men can see, but also to differences of heat.

“The iris was quite a new organ to the Grand Lunar. For a time he amused himself by flashing his rays into my face and observing the contraction of my pupils. As a consequence I was dazzled and blinded for some little time....

“But in spite of that discomfort I found something reassuring by insensible degrees in the rationality of this business of question and answer. I could shut my eyes, think of my answer, and almost forget that the Grand Lunar has no face....

“When I had returned to my place he asked how we sheltered ourselves from heat, cold, and storms, and presently why men built their dwellings upon the surface when they might excavate them. I explained that we were by no means strangers to excavation, though beside the prodigious works of the moon our own must have seemed inconsiderable. We sank mines for coal and metals, drove tunnels through hills and beneath towns, and made cellars, drains, foundations, and other works below the surface; but for our ordinary habitations we preferred the open air.

“Windows detained him for a little while. That we should deliberately pierce the walls which sheltered us seemed at first a contradiction, until I had explained glass, daylight, and our need of air. Presently he reverted to the subterranean works I had mentioned. How far, he wished to know, had we actually penetrated our globe, and what did we know of its interior?

“Here I became cautious. Our deepest workings, I told him, were scratches upon a globe nearly eight thousand miles in diameter. We knew something of the rocks we had reached; beyond them we inferred. The greater density of the earth as a whole, the increase of heat with depth, and the tremors of earthquakes furnished indications, but scarcely knowledge, of the regions beneath us. I was careful to distinguish what had been observed from what had only been conjectured.

“This distinction caused him to return several times to the boundary between what we had observed and what we had only inferred. He questioned me closely upon the limits of our knowledge, and more than once brought me back to the fact that men had crossed the space to the moon while almost the whole substance of their own planet remained inaccessible.

“‘Empty space,’ I said, ‘is considerably easier to penetrate than solid rock.’

“There was a pause, and then a movement passed through the court which I could only interpret as laughter.

“After this the questioning wandered for a time through matters I cannot now disentangle. At some direction of the Grand Lunar the quick-eyed artist who had assisted in my earlier lessons was brought forward, and presently, from my explanations, he had sketched with astonishing rapidity a rude figure of the earth, with the disposition of its principal lands and seas. I corrected him in two or three particulars, and the thing served us for a time in place of many words.

“The making of this little map led me to speak of drawing generally. I told him that men drew not only maps and diagrams, but faces, landscapes, animals, and scenes of ordinary life, and that sometimes the making of such pictures had no more practical end than the pleasure of making and seeing them. I attempted some account of painting and sculpture, likeness and composition, and presently found myself involved in distinctions I was quite unable to defend.

“‘I am not a painter,’ I said at last.

“That disposed of my difficulties more effectually than anything else I had said.

“In shifting my position I exposed more completely the deplorable state of my clothes, and so we fell upon dress. Warmth and protection were easy enough; fashion was not. The Grand Lunar wished to know what improvement was effected by altering the shape of a coat or hat merely because other men had done so. I was obliged to admit that the newer thing might be neither warmer, stronger, cheaper, nor more convenient. After one or two further questions he let the matter drop.

“Ornament and ceremony were easier, for the court about him showed that display was no mystery to the moon. From ornament we came to the music that had accompanied my approach to the Presence. I told him that men arranged sounds for ceremony and for pleasure, and that some devoted their lives to making and performing music.

“Presently the Grand Lunar checked Phi-oo and brought me back to my own occupation. If that was my proper business, how came I to know anything of pictures, dress, music, and so many matters outside it?

“I explained that among men knowledge was not distributed with the completeness he was accustomed to in the moon. A man might know a little of many things without being expert in any of them. Much came by ordinary intercourse and observation; much more from written and printed records.

“This required explanation. I indicated the sketch the artist had made. That, I said, set before the eye by means of marks the form and disposition of things. Writing did something stranger. By marks which in themselves pictured nothing, we could set down words. A man might record what he had seen or thought, and another man, years afterwards and at the other side of the world, might recover it by reading.

“Phi-oo repeated this twice, and the little eyes beneath the Grand Lunar’s enormous brain-case remained fixed upon me. There followed a rapid piping among the learned heads about his throne; Tsi-puff was appealed to, and for a little while my own words went to and fro between them in fragments. I gathered that it was not the making of marks that detained them, but this possibility of speech and knowledge existing apart from any living memory.

“I went on to tell them of letters and newspapers, books and scientific journals, and of libraries in which the words and observations of men long dead stood ranged in thousands upon thousands of volumes. By printing, too, one record could be multiplied until the same words were read by men far apart who had never seen either the writer or one another. While I spoke the Grand Lunar interrupted me only once, to have Phi-oo repeat some phrase I had used; then the cooling spray hissed over him and he remained for a time quite still. What conclusion he reached I cannot say; but for once no question immediately followed.

“I spoilt this favourable impression, if favourable it was, by adding that a record was no wiser than the man who made it. Writing preserved error as faithfully as truth; books contradicted one another; a man might mistake, or even lie, upon paper as readily as in speech.

“I made the matter worse by attempting to explain fiction. Phi-oo stopped me almost at once. ‘False reports?’ he said, after listening to the Grand Lunar. I had to make it clear that the reader might know perfectly well that the events had never happened and nevertheless take pleasure in them.

“‘And wishes to know what happened next?’

“‘Very much.’

“I confess that, put so baldly, the reading of fiction appeared less reasonable than I had ever supposed it. Bedford came unexpectedly to my assistance, or rather to my embarrassment, when I remembered his ambition to write a play. This obliged me to explain the theatre: men pretending to be other men, speaking words prepared beforehand, before an audience that knew the whole business was pretence and yet might laugh, grieve, or become excited by it. The Grand Lunar caused Phi-oo to question me upon this at considerable length.

“By now my legs were stiff, my mouth dry, and the blue light tired my eyes. A yawn betrayed me and obliged me to explain sleep. Sleep led to night and nocturnal animals, and from these the interrogation wandered away through a number of matters I can no longer set in order.

“At some point after this the Grand Lunar returned suddenly to Bedford. He had not forgotten the difficulty he had left unsettled earlier, and now wished to know what Bedford ordinarily did upon the earth. I said first that Bedford was a man of business.

“That conveyed very little.

“I had then to explain buying and selling, undertakings entered upon for profit, and the conversion, if I may so put it, of money into more money. It was in this connexion, I told him, that Bedford had regarded Cavorite very differently from myself. To me it had been principally a physical discovery; to him it suggested patents, factories, transport, companies, and wealth. From Bedford we came to money, prices, property, wages, and trade.

“The Grand Lunar still wished to know why Bedford had accompanied me. Had his presence been necessary to my work? I said it had not. Had he been ordered to come by some authority?

“‘No,’ I said. ‘He chose to come.’

“There was some difficulty over this. For several exchanges Phi-oo could get no nearer than the notion that Bedford had merely been allowed to choose between several duties. I had to repeat that no duty had been laid upon him at all. He had wished to make the journey and had made it.

“Presently he asked who determined what work men were to do upon the earth.

“I told him that, for the most part, no one did. Birth, means, education, accident, and necessity did much of the choosing for us; many men followed their fathers’ occupations, and many took such employment as they could get. But there was no authority that assigned each man his function. Within these limits men chose, competed, inherited, or simply drifted into the work by which they lived.

“At this the Grand Lunar stopped me, and Phi-oo had to repeat part of my answer. Then he recalled things I had told him earlier—fields and herds, the multitude of people in our cities, the food and materials passing continually from one place to another—and demanded how so vast a business could proceed if no directing intelligence assigned its parts.

“I tried to explain that much of it was not ordered in that sense at all. A farmer grew wheat because he expected some one would buy it; another man bought because he expected to sell again; others carried it, ground it, baked it, and sold the bread, each concerned chiefly with his own part and often knowing very little of the rest. Money, custom, law, necessity, and private advantage held these separate activities together well enough for the whole to continue.

“There was a pause.

“‘Then who directs the whole?’

“‘No one.’

“That answer had to be repeated.

“There were authorities, I explained, but none corresponding to the Grand Lunar himself. Towns and districts had their officers; nations had governments; laws were made and enforced; but no single intelligence directed mankind or even comprehended the whole movement of its affairs.

“He reached out at last to a salient fact.

“‘There is no Grand Earthly?’

“I thought of several people, but assured him finally there was none.

“What, then, stood above the government of Britain?

“I told him nothing did. Britain was one power among many.

“He wished to know whether one of these governments governed the rest. I said no. There were many independent States and Empires, making treaties, exchanging ambassadors, and managing their dealings with one another as best they could, but no authority stood above them all. What happened, then, when two desired things that could not both be had? I told him they might argue, threaten, yield, compromise—or fight.

“He returned again and again to this absence of a common authority. I told him of armies and navies, frontiers and disputed lands, commerce, rivalry, alliances, and that attachment of men to their own countries which we call patriotism. He asked whether armies existed only while fighting was actually in progress. I told him most great States maintained them in peace as well. Why? Because the other States did the same. And why did the others? Because they too feared their neighbours, or desired to be prepared against them.

“For some time the questions circled about the same point. Fighting itself was no mystery; force and restraint were by no means unknown to the moon. But among the Selenites, so far as I had been able to learn, there were no rival States at all. Each creature was bred and trained into his appointed function, and all these functions fitted together in one order. I could not say that quarrels or violence were unknown—I had seen force enough—but there were no rival powers in the moon, each complete in itself and armed against the others. It was this division of mankind into several supreme authorities, with no higher power to which all might appeal, that the Grand Lunar returned to again and again.

“He asked whether these divisions of mankind were expected always to endure. I told him some men desired larger unions, some believed nations would remain as they were, and many scarcely considered the question except when some immediate quarrel forced it upon them.

“‘And what do you expect?’

“‘I do not know,’ I said.

“The cooling spray hissed again above me. I shifted my aching knee. From the navies I had mentioned we came presently to the seas themselves, and here at least, I thought, was a matter one might describe without first explaining half the institutions of mankind.

“I gave him some notion of the oceans: water stretching beyond the horizon, coasts and harbours, islands, storms, ships, and the traffic that passed between continents. At the mention of ships he wished to know how men found their way when there was no cavern wall, roof, or fixed channel to guide them. I described the compass, charts, observations of sun and stars, and the determination of latitude and longitude. Longitude brought us to clocks and the measurement of time.

“Harbours brought in the tides. There was something exceedingly odd in sitting inside the moon explaining to its ruler the daily consequences of lunar attraction upon an earthly shore. I described the water advancing and retreating, floating vessels at one hour and leaving them grounded at another. I soon discovered that his mathematical people understood the attraction involved far more exactly than I did, and thereafter confined myself chiefly to what a man standing beside the sea might actually observe.

“From travellers and ships we came, by some turn I cannot now recover, to languages. I told him mankind possessed not one language but a great number, some related and others mutually unintelligible. At this Phi-oo was made to repeat me. Creatures of the same species, the Grand Lunar wished to know, and yet unable to understand one another’s speech? I explained that languages changed as peoples separated and mingled, and that a man learnt his speech in childhood so completely that it became almost a part of his thought. Men had even proposed artificial common tongues, but none had displaced the languages already in use.

“One answer continually suggested another question; some phrase recalled a matter abandoned half an hour before; and the Grand Lunar would pursue an incidental point until I had nearly forgotten the subject from which it had arisen.

“Navigation had brought the heavens repeatedly into our talk, and presently he returned to terrestrial astronomy. He wished to know rather more exactly what men had seen of the moon before our coming. I described our telescopes and maps, the mountains, plains, and great circular formations whose changing shadows had occupied generations of observers. When I came again to our former belief that the moon was dead, there ran through the nearer court the same faint piping and movement I had already taken for amusement.

“I defended our astronomers. Nothing visible from the earth had betrayed the world beneath the surface. We saw no clouds, seas, forests, or obvious atmosphere; no moving multitude or artificial light. The cities and galleries of the Selenites were hidden beneath the crust. Men had reasoned from what they could see, and if their conclusion had been wrong it had at least been a reasonable error.

“I tried then to describe the moon as it appears to terrestrial eyes—not merely as an object of astronomy, but as something continually present in ordinary life, rising over roofs and hills, shining upon fields and water, changing its apparent form from night to night, and entering into calendars, stories, superstitions, and poetry. Phi-oo began almost immediately to demand an exact correspondence between the figurative words and the physical thing, and I abandoned the attempt before we found ourselves once more entangled in fiction.

“For a time after this our positions were pleasantly reversed, and the Grand Lunar caused Phi-oo to tell me something of the earth as it appeared to lunar observers. Their astronomers had watched the movements of cloud upon its surface, distinguished the great divisions of land and water, and followed changes no terrestrial observer could ever see from quite the same point of view. I was astonished by the accuracy of some conclusions they had drawn at so great a distance, and no less delighted by others which were entirely mistaken.

“There is, I confess, a peculiar satisfaction in correcting the astronomers of another world.

“This part of the audience refreshed me considerably. I had spent hours reducing the familiar earth to explanations and definitions, and now for the first time I heard it spoken of as a remote body in the heavens. It was a strange thing to think that England and all the countries beyond it, the seas, the little houses and fields, all mankind with its loves and quarrels, its businesses, governments, and ambitions, were gathered there upon one distant globe. Everything that to us seemed separate and enormous—nations, cities, journeys, wars, the whole clamour of human affairs—was from the moon compressed into that small bright world, half hidden beneath its wandering clouds. For a little while I felt not so much far from the earth as outside it.

“After that my recollection becomes increasingly fragmentary. We spoke of the other planets, of eclipses and comets, and of a number of lesser matters which have left no clear order in my memory. Sometimes the Grand Lunar questioned me closely; sometimes he spoke for a considerable interval and Phi-oo gave me only the substance of his remarks.

“I had ceased by then to feel that I was being examined.

“It had become, in spite of every grotesque circumstance of our surroundings, very nearly a conversation.

“My body was less accommodating than my mind. My back had ached for a long time, my knees were stiff, and the repeated shifting of my position gave only temporary relief. My mouth remained dry in spite of the drink they had supplied me, and the bluish illumination had begun to weary my eyes. The Grand Lunar himself had been sprayed and tended continually; attendants adjusted the supports about him, and even that vast intelligence was subject to bodily limits.

“At length there was a movement among the officials nearest the Presence, and I understood that the audience was to end. The Grand Lunar conveyed that there remained much he wished to learn concerning the earth. I replied that there remained incomparably more I wished to learn of the moon, and gathered that our conversation was to be resumed.

“There followed another ceremonial performance whose meaning escaped me almost completely. The court stirred, the officials moved into their appointed places, and I attempted to rise. After sitting so long my legs proved incapable at first of anything resembling dignified locomotion, and for a few moments I stood in a most ridiculous attitude while sensation returned to them. Two attendants assisted me to my litter, and presently I was being carried away from the Presence through the blue-lit succession of galleries.

“I went from the Grand Lunar in a condition of extraordinary mental exhilaration. For all the grotesqueness of his form and the profound strangeness of the society over which he ruled, I had found in him something I had scarcely dared hope to find when I entered that enormous hall—an intelligence with which one could reason. We had misunderstood one another continually, but the misunderstandings themselves could be examined and corrected and made the starting-points of fresh questions. I had gone before him as a captured curiosity from another world and came away, or so at least it seemed to me then, almost as a visiting philosopher.

“I remember thinking, as they carried me back through the blue obscurity, that if Bedford had only remained with me long enough to see this, even he might have revised his opinion of the Selenites.

“For my own part, I looked forward eagerly to speaking with the Grand Lunar again. There were still a hundred things—”

At this point the record breaks off. The trace becomes confused for a little space and then ceases altogether.

XXVI
THE LAST MESSAGE CAVOR SENT TO THE EARTH

For a week after the message I have just given we received nothing from Cavor.

There is no reason in that circumstance alone to suppose that anything had happened to him. His opportunities of reaching the apparatus had always been irregular, and more than once a considerable interval had occurred between his communications. Nevertheless, after that account of the Grand Lunar the silence impressed me disagreeably. He had ended in so confident a spirit, so plainly anticipating further intercourse, that I found myself waiting for the continuation with an impatience I had not felt before.

I went over his account again and again. There were so many things in that extraordinary conversation which, carried a little farther, might have assumed another aspect than they had at first, and yet there was nothing in what we had received upon which I was prepared to build any conclusion. One could imagine almost anything; one could prove nothing.

And then suddenly, like a cry in the night, came the last message.

It is the briefest fragment, the broken beginnings of two sentences.

The first was:

“I was mad to let the Grand Lunar know—”

There was an interval of perhaps a minute. One imagines some interruption from without, or perhaps only a dreadful hesitation before the thing he had resolved to say.

Then, very rapidly, came:

“Cavorite made as follows: take—”

There followed one word, a quite unmeaning word as it stands:

“uless.”

And that is all.

What it was that Cavor had told the Grand Lunar and now repented telling him, the broken message does not say. I have sometimes fancied I understood him, and at other times the same explanation has seemed to me altogether insufficient. Nor can I tell why, after that first confession, his thoughts turned so urgently to the making of Cavorite. The two fragments stand together; beyond that I know nothing.

Whatever was happening about that apparatus, no intelligible signal ever reached us again.

For my own part I cannot rid myself of a vision that has grown out of these last broken words. I see Cavor somewhere in the blue obscurity of the moon, suddenly possessed by the knowledge of some consequence he had never foreseen, hurrying back to his instrument while there was yet time, striving to send across the void one last thing that seemed to him essential. I see him bending over the apparatus, intent upon the wavering signals, perhaps hearing already some movement in the galleries beyond him, perhaps hearing nothing at all.

Then the message breaks.

What followed I cannot know. I see at times the little figure of him turning from the useless apparatus, looking back once towards it, and disappearing among those vast blue-lit passages and the innumerable shapes of that strange world—passing beyond every question we can ask of him, beyond every possibility of answer, into the Unknown—into the dark, into that silence that has no end....
